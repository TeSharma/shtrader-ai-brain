# Risk Management Fundamentals
tags: risk, drawdown, stop loss, capital preservation

Risk management is the decision about how much of your account you are willing to lose on a single idea, made before the idea is placed. It is the only variable a trader fully controls: entries, exits and volatility are negotiated with the market, but risk per trade is chosen unilaterally.

The conventional survivable band is 0.5% to 2% of account equity per trade. At 1% risk, ten consecutive losses cost roughly 9.6% of the account and recovery is routine. At 10% risk, the same streak costs about 65% and requires a 186% gain to recover. Losing streaks of eight to ten trades occur naturally in any strategy with a 50% win rate, so the position size must be chosen to survive them rather than to maximise the good case.

A stop loss is a price level that proves the trade thesis wrong, not a tolerance for pain. It belongs on the far side of the structure that justified the entry: below the swing low that defines an uptrend, above the swing high that defines a downtrend, or beyond a volatility band such as an ATR multiple. Placing a stop by monetary comfort ("I can only lose $20") inverts the logic and produces stops that are hit by noise.

Daily and weekly loss limits protect against tilt. A common rule is a maximum daily drawdown of two to three times the per-trade risk, after which trading stops for the session regardless of setup quality. Correlated exposure counts as one risk unit: three long EUR positions are one euro trade in three costumes.

Capital at risk is computed as account balance multiplied by risk percent. Position size is then risk amount divided by the distance between entry and stop. Both numbers must be computed exactly, never estimated, and never produced by a language model.
